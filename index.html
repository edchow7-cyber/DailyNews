Iran and Oman reach a Strait of Hormuz accord — but reopening still hinges on the U.S.

Iran’s Revolutionary Guards said Wednesday that Iran and Oman have reached an agreement covering their shares of the Strait of Hormuz and related revenues. The agreement does not by itself reopen the waterway: Tehran says broader reopening still depends on U.S. acceptance of its conditions, while commodity traffic remains far below normal. Because the strait handled roughly one-fifth of global oil and LNG shipments before the war, even partial progress is enough to move energy prices and markets. The diplomatic opening is real, but the operational bottleneck has not yet cleared.

U.S. inflation stays sticky at 3.7%, keeping another Fed hike in play

July’s PCE inflation rate held at 3.7% year over year, well above the Federal Reserve’s 2% target, while second-quarter GDP was left unrevised at a 1.5% annualized pace. The combination leaves policymakers with an uncomfortable mix of persistent inflation and only modest growth. Markets increased the odds of another rate hike as investors looked ahead to Chair Kevin Warsh’s Jackson Hole remarks. Tariffs and the lingering energy shock from the Iran conflict remain important sources of price pressure.

The proposed $103,265 H-1B fee is now a major global tech-policy fight

The Trump administration’s proposed rule would charge employers more than $100,000 for each new H-1B visa, transforming a program that has historically cost only a few thousand dollars per worker. India’s technology industry is urging Washington to weigh the program’s role in filling specialized skills gaps, while U.S. employers are assessing whether the rule would push more work offshore or reduce hiring. A similar fee imposed by presidential action was struck down in June, so the administration is now pursuing the policy through formal rulemaking. The proposal is not final, but its scale is large enough to reshape recruiting economics if it survives legal review.

→ Reuters — What the proposed H-1B changes mean

Bill Gates is pushing for international rules around dangerous AI capabilities

Bill Gates says he wants to discuss AI risk directly with Chinese President Xi Jinping and is calling for international coordination around capabilities that could create biological threats, hack systems or cause other large-scale harm. He favors monitoring dangerous frontier capabilities without broadly suppressing useful AI development, and has floated an international body modeled on nuclear or aviation safety frameworks. The proposal reflects a shift in the AI debate from domestic model rules toward global governance, especially because the most serious risks do not respect national borders. It also raises the question of whether the U.S. and China can cooperate on AI safety while competing intensely on AI leadership.

Story 3 of 3

California’s DROP regime is now an operating requirement, not a future compliance project

California data brokers have been required since August 1 to begin processing deletion requests through the state’s DROP system, accessing the platform at least once every 45 days. Brokers must download consumer deletion lists, match them against their own records and report results back to the state. CalPrivacy is pairing the workflow with active enforcement against brokers that fail their obligations. The practical effect is to turn privacy rights into recurring, measurable data-processing operations.

Why it matters to Nera: Privacy must increasingly be executed repeatedly and audibly, not merely described in policy. That strengthens the market for systems that minimize exposure and prove how sensitive data was handled.

World Cup crowds were a transit success — and an $11 million VTA loss

Santa Clara Valley Transportation Authority says supporting six World Cup matches at Levi’s Stadium produced record ridership but left the agency with nearly $11 million in unreimbursed costs. VTA spent about $21.7 million on security and operations and received roughly $10.5 million in federal reimbursements, while fares covered only a small fraction of the remainder. The experience is becoming part of a larger Bay Area debate over who should pay for transit service around major private sporting events. With regional agencies already facing structural deficits, the World Cup is now both a ridership success story and a warning about event economics.
