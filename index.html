Houthi attacks widen the war into four Saudi cities

Iran-backed Houthi forces attacked Khamis Mushait, Abha, Najran and Jazan with drones and missiles, wounding 73 people and setting oil facilities ablaze. Saudi warplanes then struck Yemen, while more than 18,500 people have fled fighting along Yemen’s west coast in recent days. The attacks open another front around major energy infrastructure and raise the risk that disruption spreads beyond the Strait of Hormuz.

Canada’s retaliatory tariffs take effect as talks stall

Canada imposed tariffs of 15% to 50% on about $20 billion of U.S. goods after bilateral trade negotiations broke down. Britain pays a record yield to sell 30-year debt

The United Kingdom sold £4.25 billion of 30-year government bonds at a 5.8168% yield, the highest for such a sale since its Debt Management Office was created in 1998. Demand remained strong, with orders topping £85 billion, but the borrowing cost adds pressure to already constrained public finances. Rising oil prices and high global yields are making long-term funding more expensive even for major developed economies.

Story 1 of 3

Australia proposes a user choice over social-media algorithms

Australia’s proposed “My Feed, My Way” law would require platforms to let users choose between algorithmically personalized feeds and posts only from accounts they follow. Platforms would also have to document how they address harms, with penalties reaching A$109.2 million for noncompliance. The proposal follows the European approach of treating profiling as a choice that must be visible and usable, rather than an unavoidable default.

Why it matters to Nera: The regulatory direction favors architectures that can personalize or measure outcomes while minimizing exposure of raw behavioral data. Nera can frame confidential analytics as a way to preserve useful computation when consent narrows the data platforms may freely centralize.

→ Reuters — Australia’s proposed feed-choice rules

Story 2 of 3

Boards elevate digital-infrastructure concentration risk

A Capgemini survey found that large companies and public bodies are scrutinizing their dependence on critical digital infrastructure as geopolitical conflict, export controls and cyber threats expose hidden fragility. The question is shifting from whether a cloud or platform is efficient to whether an organization can keep operating when a provider, region or supply chain becomes unavailable. That makes resilience, portability and verifiable control increasingly board-level concerns.

Why it matters to Nera: Privacy-preserving computation can reduce the need to concentrate sensitive data in one provider merely to analyze it. Nera’s strongest position is not only “keep data secret,” but “preserve control and collaboration when infrastructure trust is fragmented.”

→ Reuters — boards prepare for infrastructure shocks

Story 3 of 3

Medical-device disruption puts a price on cyber exposure

Boston Scientific’s withdrawal of its financial forecast makes the cost of a cyberattack unusually concrete: interrupted global operations now flow directly into sales and profit expectations. Healthcare and medical-device environments are especially difficult because availability, data protection and regulatory duties must all hold simultaneously. Recovery plans that rely on quickly recreating large plaintext data stores can deepen both operational and privacy risk.

Why it matters to Nera: Nera’s relevance is strongest where organizations need to keep sensitive information useful across partners without multiplying exposed copies. The business case is becoming measurable in downtime and forecast risk, not only in avoided regulatory penalties.
