U.S.–Iran fighting resumes even as Tehran seeks a negotiated end

U.S. and Iranian forces exchanged attacks again after a quieter stretch since late July, pushing oil prices higher and reviving fears of a broader escalation. Iran’s President Masoud Pezeshkian said Tehran still wants a negotiated end to the war, while U.S. strikes hit Iranian launchers on Larak Island and Iran reported retaliation against U.S. bases in Jordan. The mixed signals leave diplomacy alive, but the return of direct fire makes energy markets and the Strait of Hormuz immediate risks again.

Nepal races to reach hundreds trapped after glacier disaster

Nepalese rescue teams, assisted by Chinese and Indian experts, are trying to reach 933 workers believed trapped at 11 hydropower projects after last week’s catastrophic Himalayan flood. More than 900 people have been confirmed dead and nearly 5,000 remain missing, while the Red Cross estimates over 90,000 people have been affected. The focus has shifted toward difficult tunnel operations, mass identification of victims and preventing secondary disasters from unstable glacial lakes and landslides.

Canada auto tariffs could land hardest on Toyota and Honda

President Trump’s proposed 50% tariff on cars imported from Canada is aimed at Ottawa, but Toyota and Honda may absorb much of the damage because they account for more than three-quarters of Canadian auto production. Analysts told Reuters the companies could be forced to reduce or shut some Canadian production if the tariffs take effect January 1. The episode illustrates how bilateral trade actions can spill across borders and hit multinational supply chains in ways that are difficult to contain.

Global watchdog elevates AI-driven cyber risk

Financial Stability Board chair Andrew Bailey told G20 finance ministers and central bankers that AI-driven cyber risk is the most immediate threat to global financial stability. The concern is that AI can lower the cost and increase the speed and scale of attacks while financial institutions increasingly depend on a small number of powerful technology providers. The warning moves AI security from a technology-policy issue into the core agenda of financial regulators alongside leverage, sovereign debt and market resilience.

→ Reuters — FSB warns AI cyber risk threatens financial stability

Europe orders new AI supercomputer as capacity runs short

The European Union awarded a €387.8 million contract to French state-owned Bull for LUMI-AI, a new supercomputer planned for Finland and expected to come online in the second half of 2027. The system is part of Europe’s AI Factories program, which is trying to close the compute gap with the United States and China. Demand for existing EuroHPC AI capacity already exceeds supply, making sovereign access to computing power a strategic constraint rather than simply an infrastructure purchase.

California DROP turns privacy rights into high-volume operations

California’s Delete Request and Opt-out Platform has passed 500,000 users, and registered data brokers have reported deleting tens of millions of records in response. Brokers must regularly retrieve and process centralized requests, turning a privacy right into a recurring operational data workflow rather than a one-time compliance exercise. The scale is a useful indicator of where privacy regulation is heading: measurable execution, auditability and repeatable controls.

Why it matters to Nera: High-volume privacy workflows strengthen the case for infrastructure that can process sensitive data while minimizing exposure and proving that controls were actually enforced.

California lawmakers reached a compromise on legislation aimed at regulating energy use by the state’s rapidly expanding data-center industry.
