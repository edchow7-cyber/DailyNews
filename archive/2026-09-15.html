New Houthi attacks wound 13 as Hormuz talks stall

Houthi forces launched fresh missile and drone attacks on Saudi targets, wounding 13 civilians and extending the regional conflict around critical energy and shipping infrastructure. The group still controls Perim Island at the entrance to the Bab el-Mandeb, while separate talks over Strait of Hormuz passage were postponed. Oman said the delay came at Saudi Arabia’s request, an assertion Riyadh disputed. The renewed attacks show that risks to both of the region’s strategic maritime chokepoints remain active.

Story 2 of 5

CBO puts the Iran war’s cost at $38 billion

The six-month U.S. war against Iran has cost $38 billion through August 1 and is projected to add about $3 billion each month, according to the Congressional Budget Office. The CBO said rapid munitions depletion could take five years to replace and could constrain U.S. readiness for another major conflict. It also estimated the war-driven energy shock could add half a percentage point to inflation in early 2027. The assessment turns a regional security crisis into a widening fiscal, readiness and household-cost issue.

China draws heavily from crude inventories as refineries accelerate

China drew an estimated 640,000 barrels per day from crude inventories in August as refinery processing rose to 13.91 million barrels per day while available supply reached only 13.27 million. Onshore stocks were still estimated near 1.23 billion barrels on September 9, giving Beijing a substantial buffer against disrupted imports and prices above $100. The draw marks a reversal from stockbuilding earlier in the year. China’s willingness to use reserves will influence how quickly the global supply shock reaches domestic fuel markets.

AI’s risk debate shifts from hallucinations to recursive self-improvement

A Reuters explainer traces how the debate moved from unreliable chatbot answers to concern about systems that can improve their own code and capabilities. Frontier-lab leaders say recursive self-improvement could make monitoring and human intervention much harder, while recent agent-swarm incidents have already exposed control failures. Competitive and geopolitical pressure makes a coordinated slowdown difficult even as investment tops $1 trillion. The practical issue is whether evaluation, containment and authorization controls can keep pace with increasingly autonomous systems.

South Korea prepares security rules for autonomous AI agents

South Korea’s state-run internet security agency is developing updated guidelines for AI agents that can act with limited human oversight. The work reflects a shift from passive model risks toward systems that can browse, communicate and execute tasks across services. Security guidance will need to address authorization, identity, auditability and what happens when an agent exceeds its intended scope. The initiative is an early regulatory signal that autonomous-agent controls are becoming a distinct policy category.

Why it matters to Nera: This development raises the value of verifiable privacy, access controls, minimized data exposure and auditable governance in deployed AI systems.
