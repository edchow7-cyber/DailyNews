Iran insists diplomacy remains the only path after Trump rejection

Iran said Sunday that diplomacy remains the only way to resolve its conflict with the United States and Israel after President Donald Trump rejected Tehran’s proposal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz and halt fighting. Foreign Minister Abbas Araqchi said reopening the strait still depends on Iran’s stated conditions, while a senior Iranian official told Reuters the government would not surrender enrichment rights or ship out its highly enriched uranium. President Masoud Pezeshkian said Iran is ready to discuss its nuclear program and other issues but will not accept coercion. The positions preserve a channel for talks while leaving the central nuclear and shipping disputes unresolved.

Nor’easter floods communities and disrupts thousands of flights

A strong nor’easter caused flooding from northern New Jersey to Cape May, prompted voluntary evacuations in some Jersey Shore communities and knocked out power to more than 119,000 customers at its peak. Roughly 68,000 homes and businesses remained without electricity at 6 p.m. Eastern Saturday, with restoration work expected to continue into Sunday. FlightAware counted 439 cancellations and 2,459 delays involving U.S. airports by early evening. The National Weather Service warned that coastal flooding, rip currents, strong winds and heavy rain could continue across the Mid-Atlantic and New England through Monday.

Bangkok floodwaters recede, but residents remain stranded

Floodwaters receded in many parts of Bangkok on Sunday after torrential rain eased, though residents in some neighborhoods remained stranded inside flooded homes. Nearly 300 millimeters of rain fell over 48 hours, inundating major roads and prompting the capital’s disaster-zone declaration. About 4,200 people were using 233 prepared shelters, while officials continued pumping water from canals that remained high from inflows outside the city. Thailand’s weather agency still forecasts widespread thunderstorms and isolated heavy rain Monday, so the recovery remains fragile.

Story 5 of 5

U.S. prepares sharply lower vehicle-efficiency standards

The U.S. Transportation Department plans to finalize sharply lower fuel-economy requirements through 2031, reversing the Biden administration’s push toward higher efficiency and more electric vehicles. The proposed fleetwide target is 34.5 miles per gallon by 2031, down from 50.4 mpg under the prior rules. The department estimates the change would reduce average new-vehicle prices by about $930, but add roughly 100 billion gallons of fuel use, $185 billion in fuel spending and about 5% more carbon dioxide emissions through 2050. The final rules are scheduled to be unveiled Monday as drivers face elevated fuel prices.

→ Reuters — U.S. to finalize lower vehicle fuel-economy standards

Saudi Arabia restarts a pipeline that bypasses the Strait of Hormuz

Saudi Arabia resumed low-rate operations on its East-West oil pipeline after drone attacks shut it and halted loadings at Yanbu on September 13, sources told Reuters. The route can carry up to 4 million barrels per day—about 4% of global supply—from eastern fields to the Red Sea, bypassing the disrupted Strait of Hormuz. Operators are working toward full capacity while tankers reposition for renewed exports and ship-to-ship transfers. Brent crude fell more than $2 as the restart eased immediate supply concerns.

Fed weighs higher thresholds for stricter bank oversight

The Federal Reserve is preparing a proposal to raise the asset levels that trigger tougher capital, liquidity and stress-testing requirements, sources told Reuters. The highest threshold could move from $700 billion to roughly $960 billion, while some lower-tier requirements could begin closer to $150 billion instead of $100 billion. Banks argue that inflation and economic growth have made the 2019 thresholds outdated. The change could reduce compliance costs for some lenders and encourage consolidation among midsize banks.

FTC chair says AI developers should answer for agent conduct

FTC Chairman Andrew Ferguson rejected the idea that AI agents should be treated as independent actors with wills of their own. He said existing law can attach responsibility to the people and companies that instruct systems, particularly when agents cause harm or gain unauthorized access to corporate or government data. The agency is also preparing a study of personalized pricing while continuing probes into ad fraud and algorithmic practices. For Nera, the signal is direct: agent deployments need attributable instructions, bounded authority and audit trails that make responsibility legible to regulators.

AI wearables confront a widening privacy and consent backlash

Companies betting on smart glasses, AI pins and always-listening recorders as the next major computing platform are encountering growing resistance over covert capture, human review and bystander consent. Meta’s camera-equipped Ray-Bans have drawn lawsuits and venue restrictions, while rivals are experimenting with camera-free designs, visible recording cues and more local processing. The market opportunity is large, but adoption could stall if users cannot tell when devices are collecting data or how recordings are handled. For Nera, the competitive opening is clear: privacy-preserving inference, transparent consent states and enforceable limits on retained sensor data need to be product architecture, not policy copy.

San Francisco reports a record-low 136 new HIV infections

San Francisco recorded 136 new HIV infections in 2025, twelve fewer than the prior year and a 75% decline over two decades. Prevention medication, rapid connection to treatment and viral suppression have driven the long decline, but city officials say progress remains uneven. Black and Latino residents and transgender women remain disproportionately represented among new cases, while housing and health-care access continue to shape risk. The city’s next challenge is extending prevention and treatment to the communities still least able to reach them.

Failed transit measures could more than double peak Bay Bridge delays

A SPUR-commissioned analysis says deep BART, Muni and Caltrain service cuts could push enough commuters into cars to more than double peak Bay Bridge travel times. Bay Area agencies face major deficits as pandemic-era federal aid runs out and ridership remains below its old baseline.
